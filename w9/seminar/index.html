Activity 1Small groups10 minutes

What did the teacher in the video describe about their workload?

What could be done to help teachers like this stay in teaching?

Link the account to the job demands–resources framework (demands rising, resources such as time and support) and to the Working Lives figures (74% of leavers citing workload; 26% finding workload acceptable). For the second question, draw on the retention measures and leaver evidence: workload, flexibility, support from leaders, pay.

Priority 1: financial support for entry into training

In place now

  • Tax-free training bursaries of up to £31,000 in the subjects where teachers are most needed
  • Earn while training: the postgraduate teaching apprenticeship cut to 9 months; a teacher degree apprenticeship pilot in maths
  • Help to apply: a "Find a Candidate" service for providers and an advice service for returners

Evidence from this week

  • Postgraduate trainees rose 10% in 2025/26, with STEM trainees up 21% (computing +44%, physics +36%)
  • Secondary still at 89% of target; business studies (30%) and music (65%) well below
  • NFER suggests considering higher bursaries for languages and arts subjects
Does money to start training also help people stay?

DfE (2026b), pp. 4–5, 9–12; Worth & Scott (2026), pp. 4, 10–11.

Bursaries target the entry stage of the pipeline. Recruitment also rose when graduate jobs elsewhere were weaker, which NFER links to the improvement.

Priority 2: competitive pay

In place now

  • School teacher and leader pay up by nearly 10% over two pay rounds
  • Retention payments of up to £6,000 after tax for early career teachers in shortage subjects such as physics and maths, including in FE
  • Planned: more pay flexibility for schools, and full maternity pay doubled from 4 to 8 weeks

Evidence from this week

  • Starting salaries are back to their 2010/11 real level; experienced teachers are 8.6% below
  • Average UK earnings rose 6.0% in real terms over the same period
  • Pay cited by 25% of 2025 leavers (39% in 2023) and 49% of those considering leaving
Who should extra pay go to: new teachers, experienced teachers, or shortage subjects?

DfE (2026b), pp. 8, 14–15; Worth & Scott (2026), p. 18; IFF Research & UCL IOE (2025); DfE WLTL Wave 4 (2025).

Women aged 30 to 39 are the largest group leaving teaching, which is why the plan includes maternity pay. Retention payments focus on the first five years, when leaving rates are highest.

Priority 3: protected mentoring and professional learning

In place now

  • New teachers get a two-year induction; the Early Career Teacher Entitlement started in September 2025
  • Reformed National Professional Qualifications and new inclusive teaching programmes
  • In FE: the Teacher Mentoring Programme and Taking Teaching Further

Evidence from this week

  • About 1 in 4 novice teachers across OECD systems has an assigned mentor; ~79% in Shanghai
  • 55% say professional learning improved their teaching
  • Mentoring takes time from experienced colleagues, and meetings can be lost when staff are needed for cover
What would protected time for mentors and new teachers need from a school?

DfE (2026b), pp. 13, 17–18; OECD (2025), pp. 137, 147; report sections 7 and 16.

Mentoring figures are from TALIS 2024, which England did not take part in. The point for discussion is what mentoring needs in practice: trained mentors, time on the timetable, and staffing that allows meetings to happen.

Priority 4: reduced administration and more flexibility

In place now

  • Planning, preparation and assessment time can be done at home; a new programme to spread flexible working
  • From September 2026, responsibility payments based on responsibility, fairer to part-time staff
  • Workload tools: Oak National Academy's AI lesson assistant, EdTech testing, a simpler complaints process

Evidence from this week

  • Full-time teachers work 50.1 hours in a term-time week; 26% find workload acceptable
  • 74% of leavers cite high workload; 42% cite a lack of flexible working (49% of those considering leaving)
  • Leavers describe administrative demands and difficulty keeping boundaries around work
Which tasks could go, and who would pick up the rest?

DfE (2026b), pp. 8, 15–16; IFF Research & UCL IOE (2025; 2026); DfE WLTL Wave 4 (2025).

Flexible working depends on timetabling and staffing. The report suggests asking what purpose a task serves, how its output is used, and who should do it.
