The teaching profession under pressure

Global challenges and the case of England

Today's question is whether teaching is a profession in crisis. We look at international evidence first, then spend most of the session on England. Keep in mind the difference between joining teaching and staying in it.

Activity 1 · in pairs, 3 minutes

Name one condition that would help them stay in teaching for ten years.

Are your two answers about the same thing?

Collect a few answers. Reasons to join are often about purpose (working with young people, the subject); conditions for staying are often about working conditions (workload, leadership, pay, flexibility). This difference runs through the session.

What would count as a crisis in teacher supply?

"Crisis" suggests a serious problem that existing arrangements are failing to manage. In discussions of teaching, the word can refer to several connected but different problems.

Recruitment
Too few people apply to train, start training or enter teaching posts.
Retention
Too many teachers leave a school, the state-funded sector or the occupation.
Matching
Teachers are not available in particular subjects or places, such as physics or some regions.
Working conditions
Teachers in post find the work difficult to sustain.
An education system can improve on one of these measures while continuing to struggle on another.

Report section 1.

Ask which of these a newspaper headline usually means. The report suggests that a convincing claim specifies the problem, the population affected and the period of comparison.

Teacher supply works like a pipeline

Targets describe flow

A training target concerns the expected flow of future teachers, not the number already in schools. Returners and teachers from other sectors also add to supply.

Where "leaving" is drawn

Moving school leaves an employer, not the system. Moving to an independent school leaves the state-funded sector but not teaching. A career break may end in return.

Totals and specialisms

Extra trainees in one subject cannot fill vacancies in another. Schools may use non-specialists or temporary staff, which vacancy counts do not fully show.

Long & Bridges (2026), section 2; Worth & Scott (2026), pp. 10–17; report section 1.

Losses can happen at every stage, so a rise in applications is not the same as a rise in teachers in classrooms. The third card matters later: national averages can sit alongside subject or local shortages.

Key terms used in the evidence

Recruitment
Bringing people into training or teaching posts
Retention
Remaining in a specified school, sector or occupation
Attrition
Leaving the population that a study follows
Shortage
A gap between the teachers needed and those available
FTE
Full-time equivalent: two half-time teachers equal one FTE
Cohort
A group followed from a shared starting point, such as the year of qualifying
Percentage point
20% to 25% is a rise of 5 percentage points, or a 25% proportional increase

Four questions to ask of any statistic

  • Population: who exactly is counted?
  • Question: what was asked or measured?
  • Comparison: compared with what, and when?
  • Uncertainty: sample size, response bias, or a forecast?
It also helps to check whether a result describes a level at one time, a change over time, or a relationship between two variables.

Report, "Key terms" and section 4.

Percentage points matter later for mentoring and recruitment figures. The four questions are the reading habit we practise on each chart today.

International evidence on teachers' work

UNESCO's estimate of global teacher need, and the OECD's TALIS 2024 survey of teachers' working lives.

A classroom in Japan · Darklanlan, Wikimedia Commons, CC0
Two kinds of evidence: a projection of need from UNESCO, and survey evidence from the OECD about how serving teachers experience their work.

UNESCO estimates that 44 million more teachers are needed by 2030

The estimate covers the additional primary and secondary teachers needed to achieve universal education at these levels. Around 15 million of them are in sub-Saharan Africa.

Sub-Saharan Africa · ~15m
Rest of the world · ~29m

Additional teachers needed by 2030, total 44 million

Expansion

More children need places in school, or the pupil population is growing. This need exists even if every teacher stays.

Replacement

Teachers retire, change occupation or leave. A system with falling pupil numbers can still face shortages if departures are high.

Distribution

International recruitment moves teachers between systems. Its effects depend on conditions in both the sending and receiving countries.

This is a projection of future need, not a count of current vacancies.

UNESCO & International Task Force on Teachers for Education 2030 (2024), Global report on teachers.

The global total brings together very different situations: some systems need to expand access, others have extensive provision but find it hard to retain teachers in some subjects or places. A filled post is also not the same as a suitably qualified and supported teacher.

The OECD links job demands and resources to teachers' outcomes

Job demands require sustained effort: workload, accountability, pupil behaviour, diverse learning needs and change.

Job resources help teachers meet those demands: professional learning, support from leaders and colleagues, autonomy and terms of employment.

The model connects these to wellbeing, satisfaction and career intentions, including how long teachers expect to stay.

The OECD presents this as a conceptual framework. The arrows show expected relationships, not measured causal effects.

OECD (2025), Results from TALIS 2024, Figure 1, p. 24, after Bakker & Demerouti (2017) and Granziera et al. (2020). © OECD, CC BY 4.0.

Example from the report: planning a lesson for pupils with diverse needs takes time and expertise. With a specialist colleague, materials and protected time it is manageable; without them it may extend into evenings. The framework points to staffing, time, leadership and rules as well as individual qualities.

TALIS 2024: what the survey covers

The OECD's Teaching and Learning International Survey asks teachers and school leaders about their work. Its main comparisons are of lower secondary teachers. Results were published in 2025.

Self-report
Responses describe teachers' reported experiences, such as support, workload and stress. They are not direct observations of classrooms or working hours.
OECD average
The arithmetic average of participating OECD systems with data. It is not weighted by the number of teachers, and the set of systems can differ between indicators.
Association
The survey shows relationships between variables. The OECD states that its design does not establish causal relationships.
England
England took part in TALIS 2018 but not in TALIS 2024. Evidence on England in this session comes from DfE statistics, the Working Lives survey and NFER.

OECD (2025), Reader's Guide; OECD TALIS participation lists.

A common error is to quote TALIS 2024 figures as if they described England. Another is to treat the OECD average as a world average.

Asterisks mark a higher risk of non-response bias. The 43% uses task-specific estimates, which can differ from teachers' estimates of total weekly hours.

Most teachers report job satisfaction, and some report high stress

~9 in 10

lower secondary teachers are satisfied with their job (OECD average)

19%

experience stress at work "a lot"

These come from separate questions and may include the same teachers. They are not two opposing groups.

A teacher can value working with pupils and the purpose of teaching while finding workload, pay, leadership or accountability difficult.

TALIS asks about several aspects of wellbeing: stress, time for personal life, and effects on mental and physical health. These are self-reports, not clinical measures, and differences between countries may partly reflect how people report.

Interviews with teachers in England who had left or were considering leaving also describe valued relationships with pupils alongside pressures of the job.

OECD (2025), pp. 73–76; IFF Research & UCL IOE (2026).

High satisfaction and notable stress are not contradictory. This helps explain why people who value teaching may still leave, which we see again in the English interviews and the video later.

England: recruitment, retention, workload and pay

DfE statistics, NFER's annual labour market report, and the Working Lives of Teachers and Leaders survey.

Albany Senior School, Enfield, 1943 · Ministry of Information, public domain
England is the main case. Several headline indicators have improved recently, while subject shortfalls, losses over teachers' careers and concerns about workload continue.

How England's system is organised

England, not the UK

Education is devolved. England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland differ on qualifications, training routes and early career support.

State-funded schools include maintained schools and academies. Independent schools and further education colleges are counted differently.

QTS and the PGCE

Qualified teacher status is a professional status for meeting the Teachers' Standards. A PGCE is an academic award.

An undergraduate Education degree does not automatically award QTS.

Headcount and FTE

Headcount counts people; FTE counts full-time posts. A teacher moving to part-time work reduces FTE without leaving.

Primary pupil numbers are falling, while secondary and special schools follow different patterns.

Two reports can appear to disagree when they cover different populations, units or dates.

Long & Bridges (2026); DfE, Get Into Teaching; Worth & Scott (2026), pp. 4, 8–10.

Relevant to students on this programme: a BA in Education alone does not give QTS. The FTE point matters for the 6,500 pledge later.

Routes into teaching in England

RouteIn brief
Undergraduate ITTA degree combined with professional preparation and QTS
Teacher degree apprenticeshipAn employed route with degree-level training
Postgraduate, fee-fundedFor graduates; often QTS with a PGCE
Postgraduate, salariedTraining while employed by a school
Assessment-onlyFor experienced teachers without QTS
Overseas-trainedRecognition routes, subject to criteria

Partnership

Schools provide practice and mentoring. Universities contribute research, subject knowledge, educational study and academic awards. Many routes involve both.

After QTS

Early career teachers (ECTs) receive a two-year induction. The Early Career Teacher Entitlement, introduced from September 2025, links to the ITT and Early Career Framework.

Fees, salary, personal circumstances and placement location affect which route is possible for an applicant.

Long & Bridges (2026), sections 2.1 and 3.2; DfE, Get Into Teaching.

The report notes that the implementation of early career support depends on time for both the new teacher and their mentor.

A critical perspective on teacher education

In Teacher Education in Crisis, Ellis and Childs (2024) argue that reforms in England intensified central control over training providers and constrained the contribution of universities.

Their account is informed by the experiences of teacher educators. The report presents it as one scholarly interpretation, to be read alongside the stated aims of policy documents.

It connects teacher supply to a wider question: how practical competence, knowledge of subjects and pupils, and professional judgement fit together in teacher preparation, and who decides.

Fortepan 3612, Wikimedia Commons, public domain

Ellis & Childs (2024), "Introducing the crisis", in V. Ellis (Ed.), Teacher Education in Crisis, pp. 1–26.

Here "crisis" refers to control over teacher preparation, which is a different use of the word from recruitment headlines.

About two in three teachers from the 2020 cohort remained after five years

89.7%

of teachers who qualified in 2024 were in England's state-funded schools one year later

67.4%

of the 2020 cohort were there five years after qualifying

These are different cohorts. They should not be read as one group falling from 89.7% to 67.4%. The DfE notes that the pandemic complicates comparisons between periods.

  • Cohort retention and annual exit rates answer different questions. One describes how much of a starting group remains; the other describes departures in a single year.
  • Leaving the measured sector is not the same as leaving teaching. Teachers may move to another UK nation, an independent school, another education role or a career break, and some return.
  • Experience carries knowledge of subjects, pupils and school routines. The report notes that departures can also affect colleagues who take on mentoring or extra classes.

DfE (2026a), School workforce in England: reporting year 2025 (June 2026); Long & Bridges (2026), section 2.4.

Precise wording helps: "left England's state-funded schools" rather than "quit teaching". Administrative data show who is present, not why someone is absent.

"My workload is acceptable": teachers and leaders

About three in four teachers and leaders did not agree that their workload was acceptable in 2025.

The 26% covers teachers and leaders together; the 50.1 hours covers full-time teachers only.

IFF Research & UCL IOE (2025), Working lives of teachers and leaders: Wave 4, pp. 6–8; Worth & Scott (2026), p. 20.

The two surveys give different hours because they ask different questions in different ways. On both measures teachers report longer hours than comparable graduates. The report suggests reviewing tasks as well as hours: what purpose a task serves, how its output is used, and who should do it.

Policy responses

The Department for Education's 6,500 additional teachers delivery plan, and a summary of the evidence.

Julian Herzog, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0
Policy documents set out intentions and government interpretations. The report reads them alongside independent analysis and evidence from teachers.

The pledge of 6,500 additional teachers

Additional FTE against the 2023/24 baseline, DfE 2025 workforce release 4,654 of 6,500
What is counted
A net increase in FTE compared with 2023/24, by the end of this Parliament.
Which settings
Mainstream secondary schools, special schools and further education colleges. Primary schools are not included in this count.
What affects it
New entrants, departures and changes in working hours all change net FTE, so recruiting 6,500 trainees would not by itself meet the pledge.
Reading the update
The 4,654 figure is an intermediate observation. The report notes that it differs from change across all state-funded school teachers, and that increases can have several causes.

DfE (2026b), 6,500 additional teachers delivery plan, pp. 4–7; DfE (2026a).

This slide uses the measurement points from the start of the session: FTE rather than headcount, the scope of the population, and the difference between an indicator changing and a policy causing the change.

The delivery plan groups action under attract, retain and develop

Attract

Entry into teaching

  • Financial incentives for trainees
  • Training routes
  • Support for applicants and returners

Retain

Staying in teaching

  • Pay and workload
  • Flexible working, such as part-time roles and some non-teaching tasks off site
  • Targeted incentives for subjects or settings

Develop

Growing expertise

  • Early career support
  • Professional learning
  • Routes into specialist and leadership roles
The report notes that effects depend on implementation: whether candidates can access support, whether schools and providers have capacity, and whether mentors and mentees have time.

DfE (2026b), pp. 10–18; report section 18.

Different measures address different stages of a teaching career. Mentoring also creates work for experienced colleagues, which links back to the TALIS examples.

Summary of the evidence on England

Indicators that have improved

  • Postgraduate ITT recruitment: primary 128%, secondary 89% of target in 2025/26
  • Full-time teachers' reported hours: 51.2 to 50.1
  • Workload rated acceptable: 17% (2022) to 26% (2025)
  • Starting salaries back to their 2010/11 real level
  • 4,654 additional FTE towards the 6,500 pledge

Pressures that continue

  • Subjects below target, e.g. business studies 30%, physics 78%, computing 81%
  • 67.4% of the 2020 cohort in state-funded schools after five years
  • About three in four do not find workload acceptable
  • Experienced teachers' real pay 8.6% below 2010/11
  • 74% of leavers cite workload; 73% cite stress
The report describes the evidence as supporting a qualified assessment: the picture differs between subjects, schools and career stages.

Report sections 11–20; sources as on previous slides.

The report's overall conclusion is that recruitment and retention need to be understood together, and that both improvement and continuing pressure should be described precisely.
