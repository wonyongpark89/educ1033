The teaching profession under pressure

Global challenges and the case of England

Dr Wonyong Park EDUC1033 Week 9
Dobrislava, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0
Today's question is whether teaching is a profession in crisis. We look at international evidence first, then spend most of the session on England. Keep in mind the difference between joining teaching and staying in it.

What would count as a crisis in teacher supply?

"Crisis" suggests a serious problem that existing arrangements are failing to manage. In discussions of teaching, the word can refer to several connected problems.

{{ICON:user-plus}}
Recruitment
Too few people apply to train, start training or enter teaching posts.
{{ICON:log-out}}
Retention
Too many teachers leave a school, the state-funded sector or the occupation.
{{ICON:puzzle}}
Matching
Teachers are scarce in particular subjects or places, such as physics or some regions.
{{ICON:clock}}
Working conditions
Teachers in post find the work difficult to sustain.
An education system can improve on one of these measures while continuing to struggle on another.

Report section 1.

Ask which of these a newspaper headline usually means. The report suggests that a convincing claim names the problem, the population affected and the period of comparison.

Teacher supply works like a pipeline

Targets describe flow

A training target is the expected flow of new teachers into schools. Returners and teachers from other sectors also add to supply.

Where "leaving" is drawn

Studies count leaving in different ways: changing school, leaving the state-funded sector (for example, for an independent school), or leaving teaching. Some teachers return after a career break.

Totals and specialisms

Each subject needs its own specialists. Schools often cover gaps with non-specialist or temporary staff.

Long & Bridges (2026), section 2; Worth & Scott (2026), pp. 10–17; report section 1.

Losses can happen at every stage, so a rise in applications does not automatically mean more teachers in classrooms. The third card matters later: national averages can sit alongside subject or local shortages.

International evidence on teachers' work

UNESCO's estimate of global teacher need, and the OECD's TALIS 2024 survey of teachers' working lives.

A classroom in Japan · Darklanlan, Wikimedia Commons, CC0
Two kinds of evidence: a projection of need from UNESCO, and survey evidence from the OECD about how serving teachers experience their work.

UNESCO estimates that 44 million more teachers are needed by 2030

The estimate covers the additional primary and secondary teachers needed to achieve universal education at these levels. Around 15 million of them are in sub-Saharan Africa.

Sub-Saharan Africa · ~15m
Rest of the world · ~29m

Additional teachers needed by 2030, total 44 million

{{ICON:school}}

Expansion

More children need places in school, or the pupil population is growing. This need exists even if every teacher stays.

{{ICON:repeat}}

Replacement

Teachers retire, change occupation or leave. A system with falling pupil numbers can still face shortages if departures are high.

{{ICON:plane}}

Distribution

International recruitment moves teachers between systems, with effects in both the sending and receiving countries.

UNESCO & International Task Force on Teachers for Education 2030 (2024), Global report on teachers.

The global total brings together very different situations: some systems need to expand access, others have extensive provision but find it hard to retain teachers in some subjects or places.

Demands require sustained effort; resources help teachers meet them. The model connects both to wellbeing and satisfaction, and through these to career intentions: how long teachers expect to stay. Example from the report: planning a lesson for pupils with diverse needs is manageable with a specialist colleague, good materials and protected time; without them it extends into evenings. The framework points to staffing, time, leadership and rules as well as individual qualities.

Most teachers report job satisfaction, and some report high stress

{{ICON:smile}}

~9 in 10

lower secondary teachers are satisfied with their job (OECD average)

{{ICON:heart-pulse}}

A teacher can value working with pupils and the purpose of teaching while finding workload, pay, leadership or accountability difficult.

TALIS asks about several aspects of wellbeing: stress, time for personal life, and effects on mental and physical health.

Interviews with teachers in England who had left or were considering leaving also describe valued relationships with pupils alongside pressures of the job.

OECD (2025), pp. 73–76; IFF Research & UCL IOE (2026).

The two figures come from separate questions and may include the same teachers. High satisfaction and notable stress can sit together, which helps explain why people who value teaching may still leave. We see this again in the English interviews and the video later.

England: recruitment, retention, workload and pay

DfE statistics, NFER's annual labour market report, and the Working Lives of Teachers and Leaders survey.

Albany Senior School, Enfield, 1943 · Ministry of Information, public domain
England is the main case. Several headline indicators have improved recently, while subject shortfalls, losses over teachers' careers and concerns about workload continue.

How England's system is organised

{{ICON:map}}

England, not the UK

Education is devolved. England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland differ on qualifications, training routes and early career support.

State-funded schools include maintained schools and academies.

{{ICON:award}}

QTS and the PGCE

Qualified teacher status is a professional status for meeting the Teachers' Standards. A PGCE is an academic award.

An undergraduate Education degree does not automatically award QTS.

{{ICON:users}}

Headcount and FTE

Headcount counts people; FTE counts full-time posts. A teacher moving to part-time work reduces FTE.

Primary pupil numbers are falling, while secondary and special schools follow different patterns.

Long & Bridges (2026); DfE, Get Into Teaching; Worth & Scott (2026), pp. 4, 8–10.

Relevant to students on this programme: a BA in Education alone does not give QTS. The FTE point matters for the 6,500 pledge later.

Routes into teaching in England

RouteIn brief

Postgraduate, fee-fundedFor graduates; often QTS with a PGCE
Postgraduate, salariedTraining while employed by a school
Assessment-onlyFor experienced teachers without QTS
Overseas-trainedRecognition routes, subject to criteria
{{ICON:handshake}}

Partnership

Schools provide practice and mentoring. Universities contribute research, subject knowledge, educational study and academic awards. Many routes involve both.

{{ICON:graduation-cap}}

After QTS

Early career teachers (ECTs) receive a two-year induction. The Early Career Teacher Entitlement, introduced from September 2025, links to the ITT and Early Career Framework.

Long & Bridges (2026), sections 2.1 and 3.2; DfE, Get Into Teaching.

Fees, salary, personal circumstances and placement location affect which route is possible for an applicant. Early career support depends on time for both the new teacher and their mentor.

A critical perspective on teacher education

In Teacher Education in Crisis, Ellis and Childs (2024) argue that reforms in England intensified central control over training providers and constrained the contribution of universities.

Their account draws on the experiences of teacher educators.

It connects teacher supply to a wider question: how practical competence, knowledge of subjects and pupils, and professional judgement fit together in teacher preparation, and who decides.

Fortepan 3612, Wikimedia Commons, public domain

Ellis & Childs (2024), "Introducing the crisis", in V. Ellis (Ed.), Teacher Education in Crisis, pp. 1–26.

Here "crisis" refers to control over teacher preparation, a different use of the word from recruitment headlines. Ask students to compare this argument with the stated aims of policy documents.

"My workload is acceptable": teachers and leaders

About three in four teachers and leaders did not agree that their workload was acceptable in 2025.

IFF Research & UCL IOE (2025), Working lives of teachers and leaders: Wave 4, pp. 6–8; Worth & Scott (2026), p. 20.

The two surveys ask different questions in different ways, so their hours differ. On both, teachers report longer hours than comparable graduates. The 26% covers teachers and leaders together; the 50.1 hours covers full-time teachers.

Policy responses

The government's pledge of 6,500 additional teachers: where it came from, what has been done, and how it is going.

Julian Herzog, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0
Policy documents set out intentions and the government's account of progress. We read them alongside the National Audit Office and NFER.

Where the pledge of 6,500 additional teachers came from

June 2024

Labour's election manifesto promises to "recruit 6,500 new expert teachers", paid for by ending private schools' tax breaks, including VAT on fees.

July 2024

Labour wins the election. Education Secretary Bridget Phillipson launches the recruitment drive on 8 July.

April 2025

The National Audit Office warns of uncertainties as secondary and college student numbers grow, and asks for a delivery plan.

Feb 2026

The DfE publishes its delivery plan.

{{ICON:target}}

What the pledge measures

  • At least 6,500 more FTE teachers by the end of this Parliament than in 2023/24
  • Baseline: 284,626 FTE
  • Covers secondary schools, special schools and FE colleges
  • Primary schools are left out because primary pupil numbers are falling

Labour Party (2024); Long & Bridges (2026), p. 7; NAO (2025); DfE (2026b), pp. 4–7.

The pledge counts the total stock of teachers in these sectors, so new entrants, departures and changes in working hours all affect it. Primary pupil numbers have fallen by 172,000 since 2018/19, while secondary pupil numbers plateau around 2026 and the 16–18 population grows.

What the government has done: attract, retain, develop

The DfE reports around £700 million of measures since July 2024, grouped in three areas.

{{ICON:user-plus}}

Attract

  • Bursaries, scholarships and salary grants worth up to £31,000 tax-free
  • Simpler teaching apprenticeships
  • Support for applicants and returners
{{ICON:anchor}}

Retain

  • School teacher pay up by nearly 10% since July 2024
  • Retention payments up to £6,000 after tax in shortage subjects
  • Flexible working, including planning time at home
{{ICON:sprout}}

Develop

  • Early career support
  • Professional learning
  • Routes into specialist and leadership roles

DfE (2026b), pp. 4–5, 8–18; report section 18.

Retention payments were extended to eligible FE teachers in 2024/25. Workload measures include the Improve Workload and Wellbeing service, Oak National Academy resources and funded work on AI. Mentoring also creates work for experienced colleagues.

How the pledge is going, and the criticism

{{ICON:chart-column}}

Progress reported

4,654 of 6,500 additional FTE

DfE workforce data, June 2026

  • School teacher leaver rate at 9%, the lowest since 2010 outside the pandemic
  • Secondary vacancies at their lowest for five years
  • 17,274 teachers returned to state schools in the latest year
{{ICON:scan-search}}

Criticism and debate

1

National Audit Office (2025): secondary schools and colleges face a challenge finding enough teachers for growing student numbers.

2

NFER (2026): shortages continue in science, maths and computing, and special schools and alternative provision need more deliberate workforce planning.

3

Scope: the count leaves out primary schools, where teacher numbers are falling with pupil numbers.

DfE (2026a; 2026b), pp. 4–9; NAO (2025); Worth & Scott (2026), pp. 4–9.

The 4,654 figure combines school and FE statistics against the 2023/24 baseline. Rising teacher numbers can have several causes, including demographic change and the graduate labour market, so progress on the indicator is not the same as evidence that a particular policy worked.

Summary of the evidence on England

{{ICON:trending-up}}

Indicators that have improved

  • Postgraduate ITT recruitment: primary 128%, secondary 89% of target in 2025/26
  • Full-time teachers' reported hours: 51.2 to 50.1
  • Workload rated acceptable: 17% (2022) to 26% (2025)
  • Starting salaries back to their 2010/11 real level
  • 4,654 additional FTE towards the 6,500 pledge
{{ICON:triangle-alert}}

Pressures that continue

  • Subjects below target, e.g. business studies 30%, physics 78%, computing 81%
  • 67.4% of the 2020 cohort in state-funded schools after five years
  • About three in four do not find workload acceptable
  • Experienced teachers' real pay 8.6% below 2010/11
  • 74% of leavers cite workload; 73% cite stress
The picture differs between subjects, schools and career stages.

Report sections 11–20; sources as on previous slides.

The report's overall conclusion is that recruitment and retention need to be understood together, and that both improvement and continuing pressure should be described precisely.
