A broad definition of health

The World Health Organization (WHO) is the United Nations agency for health. Its constitution was adopted in 1946 and came into force in 1948. The opening lines define health.

“Health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.”

WHO (1946), Constitution, preamble

{{ICON:heart-pulse}}

What it adds

  • Body, mind and relationships all belong to health
  • Loneliness, exclusion and insecurity become health questions
  • Health as a right: the constitution names the highest attainable standard of health as a fundamental human right
{{ICON:scale}}

Where it is hard to apply

  • "Complete" wellbeing would make anyone with a disability or long-term condition unhealthy
  • A pupil with a well-managed condition may be thriving
  • Wellbeing is not constant happiness: guidance in England separates ordinary worry or sadness from mental health conditions

WHO (1946), Constitution; Department for Education (DfE, 2025), pp. 20–21, 27.

For education, the broad definition moves attention from biological facts towards how people take part, get support and experience everyday life. A more useful question than "is this person completely well?" is what people can do, how they manage difficulties, and what support helps them take part. The DfE guidance stresses emotional vocabulary and help-seeking, while taking persistent difficulties seriously.

Health promotion is wider than giving information

In November 1986 the first International Conference on Health Promotion met in Ottawa, Canada. Its charter set out five areas of action. Education is one of them.

“Health promotion is the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health.”

WHO (1986), Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion

{{ICON:landmark}}
Build healthy public policy
{{ICON:house}}
Create supportive environments
{{ICON:users-round}}
Strengthen community action
{{ICON:graduation-cap}}
Develop personal skills, where health education mainly sits
{{ICON:hand-helping}}
Reorient health services

A school can teach about nutrition and look at the food it serves; it can teach emotional awareness and make trusted support easy to reach.

A young person may want to be more active but lack affordable options or feel unsafe outdoors. Repeating the advice adds little unless those barriers are addressed.

WHO (1986), Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion, 21 November 1986.

The Charter names five areas of action. Health education develops knowledge and skills within this wider field. The educational and organisational contributions should reinforce each other, rather than expecting pupils to overcome avoidable barriers on their own. Useful educational work explores available choices, sources of help, and the institutions able to change conditions.

Three forms of health literacy

Health literacy describes the skills people need to find, understand and use health information. In 2000 the public health researcher Don Nutbeam treated it as a measurable outcome of health education and described three forms. He revisited the idea in 2025.

{{ICON:book-open}}

Functional

Understanding and using basic health information.

For example: reading a food label, recognising a warning, following a clear instruction.

{{ICON:message-square-text}}

Interactive

Asking questions, discussing options and seeking support.

For example: explaining a worry to a trusted adult, or asking a pharmacist about a medicine.

{{ICON:scan-search}}

Critical

Evaluating claims and the conditions that shape health.

For example: comparing an influencer's claim with research evidence, or spotting a commercial interest.

Nutbeam (2000), Health Promotion International 15(3); Nutbeam (2025), Health Promotion International 40(4).

Functional: difficulty may reflect how information is presented as well as the reader's skills, so accessible communication is part of the educational job. Interactive: a pupil who remembers a fact may still struggle to explain a concern; practice with realistic scenarios helps, and assessment should include how learners communicate, not only recall. Critical: the aim is informed judgement, not rejecting expertise or expecting everyone to become a specialist.

Why knowing may not change what people do: COM-B

Susan Michie and colleagues at University College London reviewed 19 frameworks of behaviour change. They proposed COM-B as the core of the "behaviour change wheel", a method for designing interventions.

Behaviour depends on capability, opportunity and motivation, which also affect each other. Example: eating a nutritious meal.

1

Capability: knowing how to prepare the meal. This is where lessons mostly act.

2

Opportunity: access to ingredients, time and cooking facilities.

3

Motivation: enjoyment, expectations and habit.

Redrawn from Michie, van Stralen & West (2011), Implementation Science 6:42, Figure 1, p. 4. CC BY 2.0.

A person can have one component without the others. Separating them helps decide whether more information, practical support or a change in the environment is needed. COM-B helps identify what an intervention targets; it does not prove that the intervention will work. It also explains why an educational gain can be worthwhile even when behaviour change is uncertain. Families: asking them what makes advice hard to use can reveal barriers a generic message misses.

A Cochrane review of the approach

A Cochrane review pools all suitable trials on one question. In a cluster-randomised trial, whole schools are assigned at random.

  • 67 cluster-randomised trials
  • Small improvements in some outcomes: physical activity, fruit and vegetable intake, tobacco use, reports of being bullied
  • Evidence quality low to moderate; no clear benefit for every health outcome, or for academic achievement

WHO & UNESCO (2021), Making every school a health-promoting school, Part 3; Langford et al. (2014), Cochrane Review CD008958.

The whole-school question is whether pupils meet conditions that make the curriculum's messages usable and credible: food, relationships, opportunities for activity and routes to support. Programmes that rely on extra unsupported work from staff may be hard to sustain. The review supports trying coordinated approaches, while the "health-promoting school" label does not guarantee effects in every setting or on every outcome.

RSHE: how health education became compulsory in England

RSHE stands for relationships, sex and health education. The Department for Education (DfE) sets out what schools must teach in statutory guidance: guidance that schools are required by law to follow.

2017

The Children and Social Work Act 2017 gives the legal basis.

2019

Regulations make the subjects compulsory; the first statutory guidance is published.

Sept 2020

Schools must follow the 2019 guidance.

July 2025

The DfE publishes revised guidance (shown left).

1 Sept 2026

The revised guidance comes into effect.

DfE (2025), Relationships Education, RSE and Health Education, cover and paras 2, 8 (Crown copyright, OGL v3.0); Grace (n.d.), slide 17.

The 2019 regulations are the Relationships Education, Relationships and Sex Education and Health Education (England) Regulations 2019, made under sections 34 and 35 of the Children and Social Work Act 2017. The 2025 guidance updates the 2019 guidance. Keep the two 2025/2026 dates distinct: published July 2025, in effect from 1 September 2026. This is England only; Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own arrangements.

RSHE: who it applies to and how it works

{{ICON:school}}

What is compulsory, and where

  • Relationships education: primary
  • Relationships and sex education (RSE): secondary
  • Health education: state-funded schools, including academies and free schools
  • Independent schools: a separate requirement for personal, social, health and economic (PSHE) education
{{ICON:scale}}

Schools must have regard to the guidance; where they depart from it, they need good reasons.

{{ICON:file-text}}

Every school must have a written policy for relationships education or RSE, developed with parents and published.

{{ICON:users}}

Parents can ask to withdraw a child from sex education, but not from relationships or health education.

{{ICON:search}}

Key aspects are in scope for Ofsted, the schools inspectorate.

A guiding principle: participative and interactive teaching "which aims to support and not to alarm pupils" (DfE, 2025, p. 5).

DfE (2025), paras 2–5, 12–16, 21; DfE (2026), publication page.

Statutory guidance must be given due regard; recommendations within it are not the same as duties imposed directly by legislation. Sensitive topics can prompt disclosures, so staff need to explain the limits of confidentiality and follow safeguarding procedures (pp. 25–26, 39–40). External visitors can add expertise, but the school stays responsible for content. Pupils can opt back into sex education from three terms before they turn 16.

General wellbeing

“Pupils should understand that worrying and feeling down are normal, affect everyone at different times, and are not in themselves a sign of a mental health condition.”

Primary health education "starts with the benefits and importance of physical activity, good nutrition and sufficient sleep, and supports pupils to develop emotional awareness" (para 40). Other primary headings: wellbeing online, physical health and fitness, drugs, alcohol, tobacco and vaping, developing bodies. Safety appears early, before pupils face more independent situations.

By the end of secondary, health education covers…

Secondary teaching builds on primary content. Selected statements, quoted from the guidance.

Mental wellbeing

“That worrying and feeling down are normal, can affect everyone at different times and are not in themselves a sign of a mental health condition, and that managing those feelings can be helped by seeing them as normal.”

Wellbeing online

“How advertising and information is targeted at them and how to be a discerning consumer of information online, understanding the prevalence of misinformation and disinformation online, including conspiracy theories.”

Health protection

“How to navigate their local healthcare system: what a GP is; when to use A&E / minor injuries; accessing sexual health and family planning clinics; the role of local pharmacies; and how to seek help via local third sector partners which may have specialist services.”

Personal safety

“How to recognise and manage peer influence in relation to risk-taking behaviour and personal safety, including peer influence online and on social media.”

Basic first aid

“Life-saving skills, including how to administer CPR.” · “The purpose of defibrillators, when one might be needed and who can use them.”

DfE (2025), Secondary health and wellbeing curriculum content, pp. 27–31.

CPR is cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Link to health literacy: the "discerning consumer" statement is critical health literacy; navigating the healthcare system is interactive health literacy. Other secondary headings: physical health and fitness, healthy eating, drugs, alcohol, tobacco and vaping, developing bodies. The guidance asks for coordination with science, computing, PE and citizenship (pp. 32–33), and for teaching that makes responsibility for abuse clear without blaming victims (pp. 38–40).

Southampton LifeLab: a classroom inside a hospital

Running since 2008, LifeLab is a purpose-built teaching laboratory at University Hospital Southampton, run with the University of Southampton. It works with secondary pupils aged about 12–14, under the strapline “Me, my health and my children’s health”.

{{ICON:graduation-cap}}

1 · Teacher training

A one-day course at LifeLab on the research behind it, plus training in Healthy Conversation Skills to support pupils' health choices.

{{ICON:book-open}}

2 · A module in school

Eight science lessons, linked to the National Curriculum, taught before and after the visit by the class's own teachers.

{{ICON:microscope}}

3 · A day at LifeLab

A hands-on practical day part-way through the module, taught by LifeLab teachers with equipment schools do not have.

University of Southampton (n.d.), LifeLab project page; Woods-Townsend et al. (2021), Box 1, p. 6; Barker et al. (2026).

Many young people have never been inside a hospital or a research laboratory, which is part of the design. The strapline reflects the idea of a "triple dividend": benefits for adolescents now, in their future adult life, and for any future children. A life-course perspective connects present health with earlier development; a population-level association is not a prediction for one person, and adolescents' present health matters whether or not they become parents.

What pupils do on a LifeLab day

{{ICON:activity}}

“How do I know how healthy I am?”

Take accurate measurements: body composition, lung function tests, grip strength and hamstring flexibility.

{{ICON:heart-pulse}}

“What do I look like on the inside?”

Use ultrasound to study blood flow through, and the structure of, the carotid artery.

{{ICON:dna}}

“What does my DNA look like?”

Extract their own DNA and run gel electrophoresis experiments on how early-life effects may work.

{{ICON:baby}}

“How did I eat before I was born?”

Measure the transport of calcium and glucose across the placenta.

{{ICON:users}}

Meet the Scientist: small groups of 6–7 pupils talk informally with two scientists, 15 minutes each, which helps break down misconceptions about what scientists do and what they are like.

Grace (n.d.), slides 27–31; Woods-Townsend et al. (2021), Box 1, p. 6, and p. 9.

The activity titles are the questions LifeLab uses with pupils. The carotid artery runs up the neck; pupils scan each other with real hospital ultrasound equipment. The DNA and placenta work connects to research on the developmental origins of health and disease: how conditions before birth and in early life relate to health later on. A result from a practical is the beginning of interpretation: follow-up asks why repeated measurements differ, or whether one observation supports a broad claim.

The eight-lesson module

  • Theme: “How scientists work”: how research questions are formed, studies designed, and data collected, analysed and communicated
  • Based on real research, such as the Southampton Women’s Survey
  • Behaviour change techniques built into the activities
  • Taught by teachers trained in Healthy Conversation Skills
{{ICON:message-square-text}}

The approach

  • “Helping them to convince themselves – not instructing them to behave in a certain way!”
  • Promoting confidence, self-belief and self-efficacy
  • Relating concepts to everyday teenage life
  • Group discussions with young health professionals

“I went back and told Mum and Dad everything, ’cos it was all interesting.” Anna

“I found out I have a high metabolism.” Gemma

“I think Josh was the only one who was creeped out by the placenta.” Chloe

Woods-Townsend et al. (2021), Box 1, p. 6; Grace (n.d.), slides 26 and 32 (pupil interviews).

The Southampton Women's Survey is a long-running local study of women and their children's health, so pupils work with data from their own city. Healthy Conversation Skills is a training approach that helps professionals support people to make their own changes through open questions. The aim is that pupils can explain their decisions in their own terms; compliance with an instruction and understanding its reasons are different outcomes. The question for schools is how to sustain learning after a memorable day.

EACH-B: adding teacher support and an app

Engaging Adolescents in Changing Behaviour (EACH-B) was a research programme funded by the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR). It built on LifeLab to test whether a wider package could improve the diet and physical activity of students aged 12–14.

{{ICON:microscope}}

A LifeLab visit

{{ICON:message-square-text}}

Healthy Conversation Skills for teachers

{{ICON:smartphone}}

A gamified smartphone app

{{ICON:chart-column}}

Outcome trial (Barker et al., 2026)

  • 45 schools; 2,065 students at the start
  • 55% followed up after one year
  • No demonstrated improvement in the main diet or physical activity outcomes
{{ICON:eye}}

Process evaluation (Jenner et al., 2025)

  • Students and teachers valued LifeLab
  • Engagement with the app was limited
  • Teachers met practical barriers to health conversations

Barker et al. (2026), NIHR Programme Grants for Applied Research; Jenner et al. (2025), BMJ Public Health 3(2).

An earlier LifeLab trial (Woods-Townsend et al., 2021; 38 schools) found a lasting gain in theoretical health literacy but no improvement in reported diet. In EACH-B, baseline differences, loss to follow-up and pandemic disruption complicate interpretation. The result does not show that every component was ineffective. A process evaluation looks at what was actually delivered and how it was experienced; it helps separate an idea that does not work from one that was weakly implemented. Link back to COM-B: a visit builds capability; opportunity and motivation lie largely outside it.

Hazard, risk and safety

The UN Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR) publishes agreed definitions used by governments under the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015–2030.

{{ICON:triangle-alert}}
Hazard
A potential source of harm, such as water.
{{ICON:scale}}
Risk
The chance and consequences of harm in particular circumstances: weather, supervision, swimming ability, help nearby.
{{ICON:cloud-rain}}
Disaster risk
The interaction of a hazard with exposure, vulnerability and capacity. Social conditions are part of the explanation.
{{ICON:shield-check}}
Safety
Significant risks are managed. It does not mean harm is impossible.
Feeling safe and being safe can differ: vivid news can make rare dangers seem common, and familiar routines can seem safer than they are.

UNDRR (2017), Sendai Framework terminology: disaster risk.

Risk literacy means examining evidence, recognising uncertainty, and understanding why people with different experiences may reasonably notice different threats. The UNDRR definition means an event alone does not explain its consequences: who is exposed, how vulnerable they are and what capacities exist all matter.

Reading a risk claim: relative and absolute change

Health and safety messages often report a change in risk. The same change can be described in two ways.

Before: 1 in 100

After: 2 in 100

+100%

Relative change: "risk doubles"

+1 point

Absolute change: one more case in every hundred

Ask of any risk claim: which population, over what period, and which outcome?

Hypothetical example.

Both statements describe the same change. Neither alone says how serious the outcome is, how certain the estimate is, or whether the comparison is relevant. These questions help pupils evaluate both alarming headlines and overly reassuring summaries, without advanced statistics.

Who is responsible for health and safety in schools?

In Great Britain, health and safety law rests on the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, enforced by the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) and local authorities. DfE guidance (2022) explains what this means for schools in England.

{{ICON:landmark}}
Employer or proprietor
Accountable for health and safety. In independent schools, this is the proprietor.
{{ICON:school}}
School leaders
Usually run day-to-day arrangements, with competent advice, policies and staff training.
{{ICON:clipboard-list}}
Risk assessment
Identify hazards, who might be harmed and suitable controls; record significant findings simply; review when things change.
{{ICON:search}}
After incidents
Investigate, share lessons and improve arrangements.
Pupils can help spot problems, but knowing a rule cannot make up for unsafe conditions.

DfE (2022), Health and safety: responsibilities and duties for schools, sections 1–5, 12–13.

The purpose of risk assessment is action: informing planning, communicating responsibilities and checking whether precautions work. Detailed paperwork without suitable controls does not show that a risk is managed. On incidents: a rise in reported concerns can mean more confidence in reporting, while low numbers may hide problems. Combining reports with observation and people's experiences gives a fuller picture than one count.

Controlling risk: change the setting before relying on behaviour

The hierarchy of controls ranks ways of controlling a risk, from the most reliable at the top to the least reliable at the bottom.

Elimination
Substitution
Engineering controls
Administrative controls
Personal protective equipment

Most reliable at the top

{{ICON:layers}}

DfE guidance for public health incidents sets out these levels and warns against simply choosing the easiest measure.

{{ICON:megaphone}}

A reminder to be careful depends on everyone, every time. Reducing exposure through the environment protects everyone.

{{ICON:footprints}}

A school crossing: pupils learn to judge traffic, and their observations of blocked sightlines can go to the authorities who can change the road.

DfE (2022), section 5.

The broader point is to look at design and organisation alongside behaviour. Controls also need checking for new problems they introduce. In the crossing example, teaching caution stays useful but should not become a reason to ignore hazards children cannot control; responsibility stays at the right level.

Weighing risks and benefits

The Health and Safety Executive (HSE) is Great Britain's regulator for workplace health and safety. In 2012 it worked with the Play Safety Forum on a joint statement tackling misunderstandings about what the law requires for children's play.

“the goal is not to eliminate risk, but to weigh up the risks and benefits”

HSE (2012), joint statement with the Play Safety Forum

{{ICON:footprints}}

Beneficial challenge

  • Climbing, exploring, school visits
  • Build skills, judgement and independence
  • Plan in proportion: transport, supervision, access, specialist advice
{{ICON:triangle-alert}}

Preventable hazard

  • Poorly maintained equipment
  • Serious risks with no learning benefit
  • Should be fixed, not accepted
Adjust activities so pupils with different abilities can take part, rather than excluding them by default.

HSE (2012), Children's play and leisure: promoting a balanced approach; DfE (2022).

A defensible safety decision weighs the benefits of an activity against how serious and how likely harm is, the precautions available and participants' needs. Treating every visit as either unacceptable or automatically safe avoids the judgement required. A balanced approach is not a justification for neglecting serious or unnecessary risks.

Where the school safety framework came from

2013

GADRRRES, a global alliance for school safety, is formed.

2015

The Sustainable Development Goals and the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction are adopted.

March 2017

The Comprehensive School Safety framework is published with three pillars, supporting the Worldwide Initiative for Safe Schools.

Nov 2020

A review of the framework begins.

June 2022

CSSF 2022–2030 is released, revised for climate change, COVID-19 and conflict.

{{ICON:globe}}

Who is behind it

GADRRRES: the Global Alliance for Disaster Risk Reduction and Resilience in the Education Sector.

  • UN agencies (UNESCO is a co-chair), international humanitarian and development NGOs, networks and donors
  • Regional affiliates in Asia-Pacific, Latin America and the Caribbean, and West and Central Africa
  • Aligned with the SDGs, the Sendai Framework, the Paris Agreement and the Safe Schools Declaration

GADRRRES (2017), Comprehensive School Safety, pp. 1–3; GADRRRES (2022), p. 2; gadrrres.net; IFRC Global Disaster Preparedness Center (2025).

The 2017 pillars were called Safe Learning Facilities, School Disaster Management, and Risk Reduction and Resilience Education. The 2022 revision renamed the second pillar School Safety and Educational Continuity Management and widened the framework to all hazards and all risks. The Safe Schools Declaration concerns protecting education in armed conflict. The framework also aligns with the Inter-agency Network for Education in Emergencies (INEE) Minimum Standards. Regional affiliates: the Asia Pacific Coalition for School Safety, GRE-LAC, and the West and Central Africa Education in Emergencies Working Group.

The framework: a foundation and three pillars

1 2 3 4

Aims at "safe, equitable, and continuous access to a quality education for all" across every kind of hazard (GADRRRES, 2022, p. 2).

1

Safer learning facilities: sites, buildings, water and sanitation, ventilation, evacuation routes.

2

School safety and educational continuity management: risk assessment, preparedness, response, recovery.

3

Risk reduction and resilience education: for children, staff and communities.

4

Foundation: enabling systems and policies, with responsibilities, coordination and resources.

GADRRRES (2022), Comprehensive School Safety Framework 2022–2030, summary document, pp. 1–3.

Hazards covered: natural and climate-related hazards, biological and health threats (including pandemics), technological hazards, conflict and violence, and everyday dangers. The structure makes it hard to treat a lesson as a complete safety strategy: facilities, management and education are linked, and weakness in one limits the others. Child rights and resilience sit at the centre. Pillar 3 includes disaster risk, climate change, health promotion, child protection, violence prevention and psychosocial wellbeing.

Preparing children for climate-related disasters

Seddighi and colleagues (2020) reviewed research on how children are affected by climate-related disasters and how schools and families prepare them.

{{ICON:cloud-rain}}

How disasters harm children

  • Directly: injury, illness, psychological effects
  • Indirectly: disrupted care, separation, insecurity, lost education
  • Unequally: age, disability, housing, income and support shape exposure and options

“isolated school-based programmes increase disaster knowledge, but behavioural change is not forthcoming”

Seddighi et al. (2020), p. 3. The same gap we saw with LifeLab.

What helps

  • Drills that test the school's own arrangements, with debriefing
  • Plans that work across languages, senses and mobility
  • Confidence without alarm: a clear, achievable response

Seddighi, Yousefzadeh, López López & Sajjadi (2020), BMJ Paediatrics Open 4.

Link to Week 6. A family may understand an instruction but lack transport or an accessible place to go, so preparedness concerns capacity as well as knowledge. A rehearsal is useful partly because it tests the institution: a problem one child meets may mean the system needs adapting. Children can contribute observations without being made responsible for public protection failures. Note: the review includes earthquakes, which are geophysical hazards and are not caused by climate change.

How can education contribute?

{{ICON:brain}}

Capabilities

Health and risk literacy, practical judgement, and knowing how and where to seek help.

{{ICON:school}}

Conditions

Food, facilities, safe routes, risk controls and continuity plans that make advice usable.

{{ICON:users-round}}

Shared responsibility

Families, services and authorities act on what pupils notice. Children are not responsible for fixing public failures.

Education contributes most when informed judgement is backed by usable opportunities, institutional care and shared responsibility.
Across health promotion and preparedness, the pattern is the same: education builds capability; opportunity and conditions decide whether that capability can be used. The seminar applies this with COM-B and the school safety framework.
