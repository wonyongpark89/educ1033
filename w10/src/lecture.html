Health and safety education

How can education contribute to a healthier and safer society?

Dr Wonyong Park EDUC1033 Week 10 · Lecture
School crossing patrol · Pauline E, geograph.org.uk, CC BY-SA 2.0
Today's question is how education can contribute to health and safety. The thread through the session: what people learn matters, and so do the conditions in which they learn and live. A pupil may understand nutrition but have little say over the food on offer; a child may know the evacuation route but face an exit they cannot use.

Session outline

  1. 1Health, wellbeing and health literacy

By the end of the session students should be able to: define health, hazard and risk; distinguish functional, interactive and critical health literacy; use COM-B to explain why knowledge may not change behaviour; describe England's RSHE requirements; say what the LifeLab trials do and do not show; and describe the three pillars of the Comprehensive School Safety Framework. Parts 1–2 are health; parts 3–4 are safety.

Health, wellbeing and health literacy

What counts as health, why health promotion is wider than information, and why knowing is not the same as doing.

A school nurse talks with a student · Nduati.githae, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0
Four ideas in this part: the WHO definition of health, the Ottawa Charter, Nutbeam's three forms of health literacy, and the COM-B model of behaviour.

A broad definition of health

“Health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.”

World Health Organization (WHO, 1946), Constitution, preamble

{{ICON:heart-pulse}}

What it adds

  • Body, mind and relationships all belong to health
  • Loneliness, exclusion and insecurity become health questions
  • Health as a right: the constitution names the highest attainable standard of health as a fundamental human right
{{ICON:scale}}

Where it is hard to apply

  • "Complete" wellbeing would make anyone with a disability or long-term condition unhealthy
  • A pupil with a well-managed condition may be thriving
  • Wellbeing is not constant happiness: guidance in England separates ordinary worry or sadness from mental health conditions

WHO (1946), Constitution; Department for Education (DfE, 2025), pp. 20–21, 27.

For education, the broad definition moves attention from biological facts towards how people take part, get support and experience everyday life. A more useful question than "is this person completely well?" is what people can do, how they manage difficulties, and what support helps them take part. The DfE guidance stresses emotional vocabulary and help-seeking, while taking persistent difficulties seriously.

Health promotion is wider than giving information

“Health promotion is the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health.”

WHO (1986), Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion

{{ICON:landmark}}
Build healthy public policy
{{ICON:house}}
Create supportive environments
{{ICON:users-round}}
Strengthen community action
{{ICON:graduation-cap}}
Develop personal skills, where health education mainly sits
{{ICON:hand-helping}}
Reorient health services

A school can teach about nutrition and look at the food it serves; it can teach emotional awareness and make trusted support easy to reach.

A young person may want to be more active but lack affordable options or feel unsafe outdoors. Repeating the advice adds little unless those barriers are addressed.

WHO (1986), Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion.

The Charter names five areas of action. Health education develops knowledge and skills within this wider field. The educational and organisational contributions should reinforce each other, rather than expecting pupils to overcome avoidable barriers on their own. Useful educational work explores available choices, sources of help, and the institutions able to change conditions.

Three forms of health literacy

Nutbeam (2000, 2025) distinguishes three forms. Each points to a different aim for health education.

{{ICON:book-open}}

Functional

Understanding and using basic health information.

For example: reading a food label, recognising a warning, following a clear instruction.

{{ICON:message-square-text}}

Interactive

Asking questions, discussing options and seeking support.

For example: explaining a worry to a trusted adult, or asking a pharmacist about a medicine.

{{ICON:scan-search}}

Critical

Evaluating claims and the conditions that shape health.

For example: comparing an influencer's claim with research evidence, or spotting a commercial interest.

Nutbeam (2000), Health Promotion International 15(3); Nutbeam (2025), Health Promotion International 40(4).

Functional: difficulty may reflect how information is presented as well as the reader's skills, so accessible communication is part of the educational job. Interactive: a pupil who remembers a fact may still struggle to explain a concern; practice with realistic scenarios helps, and assessment should include how learners communicate, not only recall. Critical: the aim is informed judgement, not rejecting expertise or expecting everyone to become a specialist.

Why knowing may not change what people do: COM-B

1 2 3

Behaviour depends on capability, opportunity and motivation, which also affect each other. Example: eating a nutritious meal.

1

Capability: knowing how to prepare the meal. This is where lessons mostly act.

2

Opportunity: access to ingredients, time and cooking facilities.

3

Motivation: enjoyment, expectations and habit.

Michie, van Stralen & West (2011), Figure 1, p. 4. CC BY 2.0.

A person can have one component without the others. Separating them helps decide whether more information, practical support or a change in the environment is needed. COM-B helps identify what an intervention targets; it does not prove that the intervention will work. It also explains why an educational gain can be worthwhile even when behaviour change is uncertain. Families: asking them what makes advice hard to use can reveal barriers a generic message misses.

The health-promoting school: making lessons usable

WHO and UNESCO (2021) standards connect

{{ICON:book-open}}

Curriculum: what pupils learn about health

{{ICON:utensils}}

Environment: food, activity, relationships, routes to support

{{ICON:landmark}}

Governance: policies and staff capacity

{{ICON:hand-helping}}

Partnerships: families and local services

{{ICON:chart-column}}

A Cochrane review of the approach

  • 67 cluster-randomised trials
  • Small improvements in some outcomes: physical activity, fruit and vegetable intake, tobacco use, reports of being bullied
  • Evidence quality low to moderate
  • No clear benefit for every health outcome, or for academic achievement

WHO & UNESCO (2021), Making every school a health-promoting school; Langford et al. (2014), Cochrane Review CD008958.

The whole-school question is whether pupils meet conditions that make the curriculum's messages usable and credible. Programmes that rely on extra unsupported work from staff may be hard to sustain. The review supports trying coordinated approaches, while the "health-promoting school" label does not guarantee effects in every setting or on every outcome.

Health education in England and Southampton LifeLab

What schools in England must teach from September 2026, and what one local programme and its trials show.

Southampton General Hospital · David Martin, geograph.org.uk, CC BY-SA 2.0
First the national framework, then LifeLab as a case: a programme based at a Southampton hospital and studied in two large trials.

RSHE: the statutory framework in England

July 2025

The Department for Education (DfE) publishes revised statutory guidance on relationships, sex and health education (RSHE).

1 Sept 2026

The revised guidance comes into effect in schools.

Scope

England only. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own curriculum arrangements.

{{ICON:school}}

What is compulsory, and where

  • Relationships education: primary
  • Relationships and sex education (RSE): secondary
  • Health education: state-funded schools, including academies and free schools
  • Independent schools: a separate requirement for personal, social, health and economic (PSHE) education

DfE (2025), Relationships Education, RSE and Health Education, introduction; DfE (2026), publication page.

Keep the two dates distinct: published July 2025, in effect from 1 September 2026. Schools must have due regard to statutory guidance; recommendations within it are not the same as duties imposed directly by legislation. Do not describe it as a single UK curriculum.

What health education covers

{{ICON:sprout}}

Primary foundations

  • Emotional vocabulary and mental wellbeing
  • Physical activity, nutrition, sleep and hygiene
  • Personal safety: fire, roads, railways and water
  • Basic first aid
{{ICON:graduation-cap}}

Secondary progression

  • Evaluating online information; using health services
  • Peer influence, exploitation, conflict and violence
  • Health protection, substances, developing bodies
  • First aid including CPR and the purpose of defibrillators
The guidance "aims to support and not to alarm pupils" (DfE, 2025, p. 5).

DfE (2025), pp. 5, 20–31.

CPR is cardiopulmonary resuscitation. The breadth shows that health and safety involve social judgement and practical capability as well as facts. The guidance also asks for coordination with science, computing, PE and citizenship (pp. 32–33), and for teaching that makes responsibility for abuse clear without blaming victims (pp. 38–40). Delivery matters: sensitive topics can prompt disclosures, so staff need to explain confidentiality limits and follow safeguarding procedures (pp. 25–26, 39–40). External visitors can add expertise, but the school stays responsible for content.

Southampton LifeLab: practical science about health

LifeLab links schools with the University of Southampton and a hospital-based learning environment. Pupils use specialist equipment and meet researchers working on current health questions.

{{ICON:flask-conical}}

DNA extraction

Making an abstract idea visible and hands-on.

{{ICON:activity}}

Ultrasound of blood flow

Measuring blood flow through the carotid artery.

{{ICON:baby}}

Development before birth

How early life connects with health later on.

The stated aim: confidence, self-efficacy and pupils' own reasons for action, rather than following instructions.

University of Southampton (n.d.), LifeLab; Grace (n.d.), slides 22–31.

A result from a practical is the beginning of interpretation, not a complete explanation of health. Good follow-up asks why repeated measurements differ, or whether one observation supports a broad claim. On development before birth: a population-level association is not a prediction for one person, and simplified "cycles" can imply that mothers' choices determine disadvantage. Adolescents' present health matters whether or not they become parents.

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aBLZLUzK2BU · University of Southampton, LifeLab, University of Southampton (4 min)

Dr Kath Woods-Townsend, LifeLab programme director, gives an overview of the programme, its impact, partnerships and youth panel. Before playing: ask students to note what outcomes the video describes, so they can compare them with the trial evidence on the next slides.

The 2021 trial: a lasting gain in health literacy

Cluster-randomised trial in 38 schools: 2,929 adolescents at the start, 2,487 followed up after 12 months.

1 2
1

LifeLab schools scored 0.27 standard deviations higher on theoretical health literacy, after adjustment.

2

The 95% confidence interval runs from 0.12 to 0.42, so it does not include zero.

3

Reported diet quality did not improve.

Woods-Townsend et al. (2021), PLOS ONE, Table 2 (excerpt: primary outcome row), p. 10. CC BY 4.0.

0.27 SD does not mean health improved by 27%, or that every pupil improved by the same amount. Adjustment covered school clustering, gender, baseline score and deprivation. Limitations: schools and pupils could not be blinded, follow-up differed between groups, and the outcome is one particular form of health literacy. These limits do not erase the educational finding; they restrict what can be inferred about everyday behaviour or later disease.

EACH-B: adding teacher support and an app

Engaging Adolescents in Changing Behaviour (EACH-B) combined three components.

{{ICON:microscope}}

A LifeLab visit

{{ICON:message-square-text}}

Healthy Conversation Skills for teachers

{{ICON:smartphone}}

A gamified smartphone app

{{ICON:chart-column}}

Outcome trial (Barker et al., 2026)

  • 45 schools; 2,065 students at the start
  • 55% followed up after one year
  • No demonstrated improvement in the main diet or physical activity outcomes
{{ICON:eye}}

Process evaluation (Jenner et al., 2025)

  • Students and teachers valued LifeLab
  • Engagement with the app was limited
  • Teachers met practical barriers to health conversations

Barker et al. (2026), NIHR programme report; Jenner et al. (2025), BMJ Public Health 3(2).

Baseline differences, loss to follow-up and pandemic disruption complicate interpretation. The result does not show that every component was ineffective; nor does it show behavioural effectiveness. Process evidence helps separate an implausible mechanism from a plausible one that was weakly implemented or hard to sustain. Engagement with one component (LifeLab) did not carry over to another (the app). Time pressures, staffing and pupils' priorities shape what actually happens.

What the LifeLab evidence supports

Shown in a trial

Educational outcome

Theoretical health literacy improved and lasted twelve months.

Not shown

Behavioural outcome

Diet and physical activity did not improve in either trial.

Not measured

Health outcome

Effects on later disease would need long follow-up.

Understanding evidence, confidence in asking questions and critical reflection are educational gains in their own right.

Woods-Townsend et al. (2021); Barker et al. (2026); Jenner et al. (2025).

Each level needs its own evidence. LifeLab offers evidence of improved theoretical health literacy and an engaging setting for practical science. Claims about lasting behaviour change need more caution. A balanced judgement keeps the educational contribution while asking about access, delivery costs, teacher support, and the conditions that might let learning shape daily routines. Link back to COM-B: health literacy is capability; opportunity and motivation were outside the visit.

Risk and school health and safety

Hazard and risk, who is responsible in schools, and how to control risk without removing every challenge.

Fire drill, Baozhong Junior High School, Taiwan · Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0
We move from health to safety. The same theme continues: what pupils know matters, and so do the conditions the institution provides.

Hazard, risk and safety

{{ICON:triangle-alert}}
Hazard
A potential source of harm, such as water.
{{ICON:scale}}
Risk
The chance and consequences of harm in particular circumstances: weather, supervision, swimming ability, help nearby.
{{ICON:cloud-rain}}
Disaster risk
The interaction of a hazard with exposure, vulnerability and capacity. Social conditions are part of the explanation.
{{ICON:shield-check}}
Safety
Significant risks are managed. It does not mean harm is impossible.
Feeling safe and being safe can differ: vivid news can make rare dangers seem common, and familiar routines can seem safer than they are.

United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR, 2017), terminology: disaster risk.

Risk literacy means examining evidence, recognising uncertainty, and understanding why people with different experiences may reasonably notice different threats. The UNDRR definition means an event alone does not explain its consequences: who is exposed, how vulnerable they are and what capacities exist all matter.

Reading a risk claim: relative and absolute change

Before: 1 in 100

After: 2 in 100

+100%

Relative change: "risk doubles"

+1 point

Absolute change: one more case in every hundred

Ask of any risk claim: which population, over what period, and which outcome?

Hypothetical example.

Both statements describe the same change. Neither alone says how serious the outcome is, how certain the estimate is, or whether the comparison is relevant. These questions help pupils evaluate both alarming headlines and overly reassuring summaries, without advanced statistics.

Who is responsible for health and safety in schools?

{{ICON:landmark}}
Employer or proprietor
Accountable for health and safety. In independent schools, this is the proprietor.
{{ICON:school}}
School leaders
Usually run day-to-day arrangements, with competent advice, policies and staff training.
{{ICON:clipboard-list}}
Risk assessment
Identify hazards, who might be harmed and suitable controls; record significant findings simply; review when things change.
{{ICON:search}}
After incidents
Investigate, share lessons and improve arrangements.
Pupils can help spot problems, but knowing a rule cannot make up for unsafe conditions.

DfE (2022), Health and safety: responsibilities and duties for schools, sections 1–5, 12–13.

The purpose of risk assessment is action: informing planning, communicating responsibilities and checking whether precautions work. Detailed paperwork without suitable controls does not show that a risk is managed. On incidents: a rise in reported concerns can mean more confidence in reporting, while low numbers may hide problems. Combining reports with observation and people's experiences gives a fuller picture than one count.

Controlling risk: change the setting before relying on behaviour

The hierarchy of controls

Elimination
Substitution
Engineering controls
Administrative controls
Personal protective equipment

Most reliable at the top

{{ICON:layers}}

DfE guidance for public health incidents sets out these levels and warns against simply choosing the easiest measure.

{{ICON:megaphone}}

A reminder to be careful depends on everyone, every time. Reducing exposure through the environment protects everyone.

{{ICON:footprints}}

A school crossing: pupils learn to judge traffic, and their observations of blocked sightlines can go to the authorities who can change the road.

DfE (2022), section 5.

The broader point is to look at design and organisation alongside behaviour. Controls also need checking for new problems they introduce. In the crossing example, teaching caution stays useful but should not become a reason to ignore hazards children cannot control; responsibility stays at the right level.

Weighing risks and benefits

“the goal is not to eliminate risk, but to weigh up the risks and benefits”

Health and Safety Executive (HSE, 2012), joint statement with the Play Safety Forum

{{ICON:footprints}}

Beneficial challenge

  • Climbing, exploring, school visits
  • Build skills, judgement and independence
  • Plan in proportion: transport, supervision, access, specialist advice
{{ICON:triangle-alert}}

Preventable hazard

  • Poorly maintained equipment
  • Serious risks with no learning benefit
  • Should be fixed, not accepted
Adjust activities so pupils with different abilities can take part, rather than excluding them by default.

HSE (2012), Children's play and leisure: promoting a balanced approach; DfE (2022).

A defensible safety decision weighs the benefits of an activity against how serious and how likely harm is, the precautions available and participants' needs. Treating every visit as either unacceptable or automatically safe avoids the judgement required. A balanced approach is not a justification for neglecting serious or unnecessary risks.

Comprehensive school safety and climate disruption

A global framework for school safety, climate-related disruption in 2024, and preparedness. This builds on Week 6.

Flooding during torrential rain, Baozhong Junior High School, Taiwan · Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0
Week 6 looked at disaster education through Grenfell and the 2011 Japan earthquake. Here we zoom out to a framework for the whole education system, and to climate-related disruption.

The Comprehensive School Safety Framework 2022–2030

1 2 3 4

Aims at "safe, equitable, and continuous access to a quality education for all" across every kind of hazard (GADRRRES, 2022, p. 2).

1

Safer learning facilities: sites, buildings, water and sanitation, ventilation, evacuation routes.

2

School safety and educational continuity management: risk assessment, preparedness, response, recovery.

3

Risk reduction and resilience education: for children, staff and communities.

4

Foundation: enabling systems and policies, with responsibilities, coordination and resources.

Global Alliance for Disaster Risk Reduction and Resilience in the Education Sector (GADRRRES, 2022), summary document, pp. 1–3.

Hazards covered: natural and climate-related hazards, biological threats, technological hazards, conflict, violence and everyday dangers. The structure makes it hard to treat a lesson as a complete safety strategy: facilities, management and education are linked, and weakness in one limits the others. Child rights and resilience sit at the centre. Pillar 3 includes disaster risk, climate change, health promotion, child protection, violence prevention and psychosocial wellbeing. The seminar includes a short GADRRRES video on the framework.

Educational continuity means more than reopening

Can every pupil actually learn?

{{ICON:wifi-off}}

Assumptions about internet access, quiet space, language or adult help can exclude families.

{{ICON:accessibility}}

The framework names barriers linked to disability, digital inequality and social exclusion.

{{ICON:message-square-text}}

Plans need accessible materials, several communication channels and contact with those the default misses.

Resilience and institutional change

{{ICON:refresh-ccw}}

Capacities to anticipate, absorb, adapt and transform.

{{ICON:building-2}}

Recurring overheating may need changes to buildings and routines, not only advice to pupils.

{{ICON:shield-alert}}

Resilience should not mean pupils and teachers putting up with unsafe conditions.

GADRRRES (2022), summary document.

Transformation matters when an institution meets the same harm again and again. People's capacity to cope depends partly on resources, decisions and support beyond their control. Overheating returns in the seminar as the scenario for Activity 2.

Climate-related disruption to schooling in 2024

1 2

Students affected by climate-related school disruption, by month. Icons show the main hazard each month.

1

April was the largest month: 123 million students, mostly affected by heatwaves.

2

Monthly totals cannot be added up: some students were disrupted more than once. Without duplicates: at least 242 million students in 85 countries.

UNICEF (2025), Learning interrupted: global snapshot of climate-related school disruptions in 2024, Figure 1, p. 3.

This is an estimate for one year, not an annual constant or a count of permanently lost places. Disruption shows how much depends on facilities, transport, communication and alternative provision. Effects can last after buildings reopen, especially where families have lost resources.

Preparing children for climate-related disasters

{{ICON:cloud-rain}}

How disasters harm children

  • Directly: injury, illness, psychological effects
  • Indirectly: disrupted care, separation, insecurity, lost education
  • Unequally: age, disability, housing, income and support shape exposure and options

“isolated school-based programmes increase disaster knowledge, but behavioural change is not forthcoming”

Seddighi et al. (2020), p. 3. The same gap we saw with LifeLab.

What helps

  • Drills that test the school's own arrangements, with debriefing
  • Plans that work across languages, senses and mobility
  • Confidence without alarm: a clear, achievable response

Seddighi, Yousefzadeh, López López & Sajjadi (2020), BMJ Paediatrics Open 4.

Link to Week 6. A family may understand an instruction but lack transport or an accessible place to go, so preparedness concerns capacity as well as knowledge. A rehearsal is useful partly because it tests the institution: a problem one child meets may mean the system needs adapting. Children can contribute observations without being made responsible for public protection failures. Note: the review includes earthquakes, which are geophysical hazards and are not caused by climate change.

Type of sourceWhat it can tell usThis week
Statutory guidanceWhat schools are expected to doDfE (2025) RSHE; DfE (2022)
FrameworkHow to organise actionGADRRRES (2022); WHO & UNESCO (2021)
ReviewWhat a body of studies suggestsLangford et al. (2014); Seddighi et al. (2020)

Process evaluationWhat was delivered and how it was experiencedJenner et al. (2025)
A broad framework is not proof that each part works; an inconclusive behaviour trial does not show that nothing was learned.
Evaluation also needs to say which outcome it is about: educational (knowledge, interpretation, communication, judgement), behavioural (what people do over time) or health (illness, injury, wellbeing). Attendance and enjoyment show participation, not learning. Averages can hide barriers for particular groups, so look at who took part and who is missing.

How can education contribute?

{{ICON:brain}}

Capabilities

Health and risk literacy, practical judgement, and knowing how and where to seek help.

{{ICON:school}}

Conditions

Food, facilities, safe routes, risk controls and continuity plans that make advice usable.

{{ICON:users-round}}

Shared responsibility

Families, services and authorities act on what pupils notice. Children are not responsible for fixing public failures.

Education contributes most when informed judgement is backed by usable opportunities, institutional care and shared responsibility.
Across health promotion and preparedness, the pattern is the same: education builds capability; opportunity and conditions decide whether that capability can be used. The seminar applies this with COM-B and the school safety framework.
