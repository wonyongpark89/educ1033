Recap: why knowing may not change what people do

Capability
Knowledge and practical skills
Opportunity
Physical circumstances (time, money, places, equipment) and social ones (friends, family, norms)
Motivation
Reflective intentions, and automatic processes such as habit and enjoyment
Lessons mostly build capability. Opportunity and motivation often depend on conditions outside the lesson.

Michie, van Stralen & West (2011), Figure 1, p. 4. CC BY 2.0.

Remind students of the meal example from the lecture: knowing how to cook (capability), access to ingredients and a kitchen (opportunity), enjoyment and habit (motivation). This model is the tool for Activity 1.

Nutbeam (2000, 2025); Woods-Townsend et al. (2021); Barker et al. (2026); Jenner et al. (2025).

The LifeLab case shows the pattern COM-B predicts: a lasting gain in understanding (capability) without measured change in behaviour. That does not make the learning worthless; it means behaviour also depends on opportunity and motivation.

Recap: school safety is more than a lesson

Responsibility

The employer or proprietor is accountable. Pupils can help spot problems, but knowing a rule cannot make up for unsafe conditions.

Hierarchy of controls

Elimination, substitution and engineering controls before administrative controls and protective equipment. Change the setting before relying on behaviour.

Three pillars

Safer facilities; safety and continuity management; risk reduction and resilience education, on a foundation of systems and policies.

DfE (2022), sections 1–5; GADRRRES (2022), pp. 1–3.

These three ideas set up Activity 2. The video next goes through the framework's three pillars in more detail.

Scenario: healthy eating in a Year 9 tutor group

Pupils have had lessons on balanced diets and can explain them. Six things they say about what they actually eat:

A

"We learned about balanced meals, but I've never actually cooked one."

B

"The lunch queue takes most of the break, so I grab something on the way in."

C

"My parents work late, and there's not much in to cook with."

D

"Everyone in my group gets the same snack after school. It's just what we do."

E

"I know fruit is better for me. I just don't fancy it."

F

"The healthier options at the shop near us cost more."

Hypothetical scenario, built from the barriers described in Michie et al. (2011) and WHO (1986).

Read the statements aloud or give groups a minute to read. Possible sorting (accept reasoned alternatives): A capability (practical skill); B physical opportunity (time, school organisation); C physical opportunity (home resources, time); D social opportunity and automatic motivation (habit, peers); E motivation (enjoyment); F physical opportunity (cost). Several statements fit more than one component, which is the point: barriers interact.
Activity 1Groups of 3–415 minutes

Sort statements A–F into capability, opportunity and motivation. Some may fit more than one.

Which barriers could another lesson address, and which need the school, families or others to change something?

Propose one change the school could make that does not rely on pupils trying harder.

About 10 minutes in groups, 5 for feedback. Prompts: B (queue) is about school organisation, e.g. lunch timing or serving points. C and F concern home resources and cost; the school might offer practical cooking with ingredients provided, or signpost support, without assuming families are indifferent. D suggests working with peer norms rather than individual advice. Link back to the Ottawa Charter: personal skills alongside supportive environments. Ask groups how they would know whether their change worked (educational, behavioural or health outcome?).

Scenario: a school facing repeated hot days

The school

  • A secondary school has several very hot days each summer term
  • Top-floor classrooms overheat; the playing field has little shade
  • Summer exams take place in the sports hall
  • Some pupils travel long distances on foot or by bus
  • Letters home go out by one app, in English

From the sources

  • In April 2024, 123 million students worldwide had schooling disrupted, mostly by heatwaves (UNICEF, 2025)
  • Recurring overheating may need changes to buildings and routines, not only advice (GADRRRES, 2022)
  • Continuity plans should ask which pupils face extra barriers and how families receive information

Hypothetical scenario. UNICEF (2025), Figure 1, p. 3; GADRRRES (2022).

The scenario details are invented for the activity; the right-hand column comes from the week's sources. Draw attention to the last two school details: travel and communication are where equity questions usually appear.
Activity 2Groups of 3–420 minutes

Plan the school's response to repeated hot days. Give at least one action under each pillar:

1 · Safer learning facilities

2 · Safety and continuity management

3 · Risk reduction and resilience education

  1. aWhich pupils might face extra barriers, and how does your plan reach them?
  2. bWhich actions rely on pupils' behaviour, and which change the setting?
About 13 minutes in groups and 7 for feedback; ask each group for one action per pillar and build a shared list. Possible answers. Pillar 1: shade, blinds, ventilation, water points, moving lessons or exams out of the hottest rooms. Pillar 2: a hot-weather plan with trigger points, adjusted timetables and uniform rules, who decides and how families are told (several channels and languages). Pillar 3: lessons on heat and wellbeing, recognising symptoms, how to ask for help. Barriers: pupils with health conditions or disabilities, long journeys, families who do not use the app or read English. Question b links to the hierarchy of controls: advice to drink water relies on behaviour; water points and shade change the setting. Pupils' observations (which rooms are hottest) can inform the plan without making them responsible for it.

Bringing the two activities together

In both scenarios, lessons build capability: knowing about diet, or about heat and wellbeing.

Whether that knowledge can be used depends on opportunity: lunch queues, cost, shade, water, timetables.

Many of those conditions are the institution's responsibility, shared with families and services.

Exit question

Name one thing a school, not a pupil, should be responsible for in health or safety, and say why.

Michie et al. (2011); WHO (1986); GADRRRES (2022).

Collect a few answers aloud or on paper. This connects the lecture's conclusion: education contributes most when informed judgement is backed by usable opportunities, institutional care and shared responsibility.
